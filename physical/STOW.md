A dangling arm is 80 mm long and ends in a bare plug 22.7 mm wide, so it can hit a neighbour. A folded-back arm is not possible (the hinge turns ±50° and its slot opens ±82°). Two answers:

## Option 1: leave the arm on and click it out of the way

No hardware at all. Every tree was laid out again with the unused arms present, each clicked to whichever of the five positions keeps it clear, and its bare plug hanging off the end:

It works for **8 of 10** trees, with trees up to about 40 mm wider. The unused arms click to the outside (left arms to −50° or −25°, right to +25°) and middle arms to 0° or −25°. The independent distance check found no clashes.
The 2 that fail are the biggest X′ trees. The failure is at the root, CP: the parked arms deep in its subtrees stick out far enough that CP's own two arms (80 mm at ±50°) can no longer spread its children apart. Taking those unused arms off fixes it.

## Option 2: arms that snap in and come off

Because it works for most trees and not all, and because a bare plug hanging off an arm will snag, the kit should also let any unused arm be removed. Every arm is the same part, so this also means a three-slot piece does not need three arms.

**The joint.** The hub has a rectangular socket cut in from its rim, running past its centre. The arm's root is a tongue split down the middle into two prongs, each with a small barb on its outer face; the barbs click into dimples in the socket walls. The arm seats against the hub rim on a concave shoulder, so it always goes in to the same depth. Because the tongue is square, the arm cannot turn in the hub: the hinge torque passes through the socket walls, and the detent grooves are on the hub's end faces, clear of the socket.

| | |
|---|---|
| arm | 5 mm wide × 8 mm thick, 80 mm from the hinge axis (the arm in the hinge test was 9 mm thick; this one is 8 so the socket clears the grooves) |
| socket | 5.2 × 8.2 mm, from the hub rim to 3.2 mm past the axis |
| barbs | 0.35 mm high, flank slope 0.55, 8 mm long; each has to push its prong in by 0.25 mm to get past the socket wall |
| housing | the arm slot is 1 mm higher (`hinge_housing_q.stl`) |

How hard it pulls off is set by the prong thickness; three arms are provided to find the right feel (calculated for printed PETG, μ = friction):

| Prong thickness | slit | stiffness each | stress | strain | pull-off at μ 0 | μ 0.3 | μ 0.45 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 mm | 2.0 mm | 47 N/mm | 23 MPa | 1.6% | 13 N | 24 N | 31 N |
| 1.3 mm | 2.4 mm | 31 N/mm | 20 MPa | 1.4% | 8 N | 16 N | 20 N |
| 1.1 mm | 2.8 mm | 18 N/mm | 17 MPa | 1.1% | 5 N | 9 N | 12 N |

What it has to resist is the weight of a hanging phrase pulling along the arm, a few newtons (the heaviest, 350 g, is 3.4 N), so about 10 to 15 N of pull-off is comfortable and a hand can still do it. These are **calculated, not measured**, and the barb is only 0.35 mm high, which is small for a 0.4 mm nozzle; the three thicknesses are there to find out.

### What the script checked on the real meshes

- All five parts are watertight solids.
- With the arm on, **the hub still turns freely through ±55°** (overlap 0.000 mm³) and touches at ±60°.
- Pushing the arm in, the barbs interfere with the socket walls by **1.72 mm³ at most** (predicted from the barb shape: 1.82); seated, there is no interference; 13 mm out the arm is clear of the hub.
- At the −50°, 0° and +50° clicks the arm can be **pulled straight out with nothing in the way** (overlap with housing and lids 0.000 mm³).

## A problem found on the way: children tilt with their arm

The arm layout assumed that a child hangs **upright** from the end of its arm. A plug that is fixed to the arm would instead tilt the child by the arm's angle, and its own arms with it. The same trees laid out with rigid, tilting children:

| Tree | children stay upright | children tilt with the arm |
|---|---|---|
| XP · L1 four-pieces | 266 × 315 mm | 504 × 242 mm |
| XP · L1 one-more-branch | 376 × 406 mm | 651 × 258 mm |
| XP · L2 the-cat | 96 × 132 mm | 105 × 132 mm |
| XP · L2 the-fluffy-cat-will-jump | 247 × 257 mm | 413 × 209 mm |
| X′ · L1 four-pieces | 188 × 322 mm | 320 × 279 mm |
| X′ · L1 one-more-branch | 527 × 1051 mm | 1179 × 1171 mm |
| X′ · L2 the-cat | 126 × 319 mm | 149 × 317 mm |
| X′ · L2 the-cat-chased-the-mouse | 346 × 692 mm | 987 × 344 mm |
| X′ · L2 did-the-cat-chase-the-mouse | 346 × 896 mm | 885 × 630 mm |
| X′ · L2 which-mouse-did-the-cat-chase | 449 × 868 mm | 863 × 989 mm |

They still lay out without collisions, but the trees sprawl and bend sideways (the biggest becomes about 1.2 m square) and stop looking like the trees in the game. So the arm-end joint cannot be a rigid plug. The natural fix is a **swivel at the arm tip** that carries the plug, so a hanging child stays plumb (and can be given a light detent at upright). That is a design of its own, and it changes how the arm ends.

## What this does not settle

- **The tilt problem above.** It matters more than stowing; every layout result in `ARMS.md` assumes upright children.
- **Where removed arms live.** They need a tray in the box; arms are the one loose part in the system.
- **How many arms a kit needs.** Fewer than three per three-slot piece, since unused ones come off. The most hinged arms any one of the game's trees uses is 28, so a kit that can build any of them needs at least that many arms.
- **The snap itself.** The numbers are predictions; the three arms in the set are for finding the right feel on a real printer.
